The product

Food Profile

A centralized preference system where customers define and manage their food tastes — powering ML-driven personalization across HelloFresh's entire recipe menu.

Customers can set diet preferences, define household composition, set hard restrictions (diet type, allergies), and express taste preferences — all of which immediately adjust the menu ranking.

View prototype

Context

The product & the team

HelloFresh is the world's largest meal-kit company, serving approximately 8 million customers globally.

As the menu expanded from ~100 → 500+ recipes per week, strong personalization became critical. Without it, customers face an overwhelming, irrelevant choice.

A new Personalization team was formed to close this gap. I joined as the sole designer, leading Customer Profile UX end-to-end.

The problem

Before: one size fits all

Every HelloFresh customer received the exact same experience regardless of who they were.

Menu personalization came down to a single generic preference dropdown — a handful of broad categories. Everyone got the same menu ranking and the same pre-selected meals.

"HelloFresh knew your delivery address but not that you don't eat fish."

For loyal customers ordering every week for months or years, this was increasingly frustrating. No way to tell HelloFresh what they loved, what they'd never eat, or what kind of cooking they wanted to do.

Previous experience — single preference dropdown

The problem

The business impact of no personalization

📈

Menu scaling pressure

100 → 500+ recipes per week. Without personalization, customers face overwhelming choice with no relevance signal.

🤖

ML had no explicit signal

Implicit behavioral data alone was insufficient. The ML team needed explicit preference input to train and rank recipes effectively.

📉

Engagement & retention risk

Loyal customers felt unseen. Irrelevant menus erode trust and increase churn likelihood for long-term subscribers.

Food Profile was the response: give customers a voice, give the ML team a signal.

Research

How do customers think about food?

I partnered with the UX research team on a card sorting exercise to understand how customers mentally grouped food preferences, subscription settings, and related options.

Key finding: customers' mental model differed significantly from the product team's assumptions. Those findings directly shaped the structure of Food Profile's categories.

Also conducted

  • Concept testing — 3 directions explored
  • Usability sessions before release
  • Post-launch Sprig session recordings
  • Moderated personalization study (in planning)

Card sorting results — category clustering

System thinking

How personalization works

Every design decision about what questions to collect was tied directly to a personalization signal. Nothing was included without an ML use case.

Explicit signals — from Food Profile

Diet type · Allergies · Household size · Cuisines · Proteins · Dish types

Implicit signals — from behavior

Recipes ordered · Recipes skipped · Weekly selections · Meal ratings

Output

ML scores recipes → dynamically ranks the menu → pre-selects weekly meals. All recipes remain available — no over-filtering.

Design process

What to collect & how to structure it

Structured from hardest restrictions to softest preferences — matching both the user's mental model and ML signal priority.

1

Hard restrictions

Diet type · Allergies — safety-critical, highest ML signal value

2

Household context

Household size & composition — servings and family makeup

3

Taste preferences

Proteins · Cuisines · Dish types — flexible, personalization-enhancing

Full user journey

Entry points, happy path & edge cases

Entry points

  • Account settings → Food Profile page
  • Post-meal-rating prompt (in progress)
  • Meal selection contextual banner (in progress)

Happy path

  • User opens Food Profile, completes all categories
  • Saves — personalization applies immediately
  • Menu re-ranks in real time on next visit

Edge cases

  • Partial completion — personalization works with partial data
  • Conflicting selections (vegan + seafood) — graceful, no blocking
  • Skipping all questions — zero-state, no empty UI
  • Long translation strings — localization-tested, 15+ languages

Error states

  • Save failure — sticky bar with retry + error message
  • Connectivity loss — local state preserved, save queued
  • Allergy conflict with plan — user informed, not blocked

Design iterations

How design evolved through the process

Phase 1

Early rapid prototyping — AI-assisted

Explored multiple IA structures and interaction patterns using Figma Make + Vercel. Arrived at team discussions with 3 directions, not 1.
